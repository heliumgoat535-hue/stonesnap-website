Best Rock Identifier Apps for Android (2026)

Not all rock identifier apps work the same way. Some use outdated visual matching databases, others run large AI models that understand geological context. Here's what separates them.

Rock identification used to mean carrying a worn field guide, scraping rocks across ceramic tiles, and making educated guesses. Now you point your phone at a specimen and an AI gives you its mineral composition, crystal system, and Mohs hardness in under five seconds. The technology has matured enough to be genuinely useful — but not every rock identifier app delivers on that promise.

This comparison covers every significant rock identifier app currently available on Google Play, tested against the same set of specimens: common igneous rocks, sedimentary formations, metamorphic samples, and a handful of minerals and crystals. We looked at identification accuracy, data quality, interface design, and value.

What Makes a Good Rock Identifier App?

Before ranking, it's worth establishing what "good" means for a rock identification app. There are three things that matter:

  • Identification accuracy: Does it correctly name the specimen from a photo? This is the obvious one, and it varies enormously between apps.
  • Data quality: After identification, does it give you useful geological information — Mohs hardness, chemical formula, formation environment, where it's commonly found? A correct name with no context is barely useful.
  • Practical usability: Can you use it in the field on a phone screen in sunlight? Is the camera interface fast enough to capture a specimen without frustration?

AI-powered rock identifier apps that use large vision models (like Google's Gemini) have a structural advantage here: they were trained on vast image datasets that include geological specimens across lighting conditions, angles, and states of weathering. Apps that rely on smaller, purpose-built classifiers or simple image matching tend to struggle with anything outside their training set.

2. Rock Identifier — Stone ID (Various Publishers)

There are several apps on Google Play using names like "Rock Identifier," "Stone Identifier," or "Rock ID" from various publishers. Most use AI image classification, though the underlying models vary in capability.

The better versions of these apps identify common rocks reasonably well — granite, basalt, quartz, and sandstone are usually identified correctly from clear photos. They tend to struggle more with metamorphic rocks, weathered specimens, and anything geologically unusual.

Data quality is typically the weakness. Many of these apps return an identification and a short description that amounts to a Wikipedia summary — generic enough to be technically accurate but not particularly useful for anyone doing serious collecting or studying. Information like precise Mohs hardness ranges, crystal system, formation environment, and associated minerals is often absent.

Best for: Casual users who only need to know the name of a common specimen and aren't looking for geological depth.

3. iNaturalist

iNaturalist is a species identification app used primarily for identifying plants, insects, fungi, and animals. It does accept rock and mineral submissions, and the community of naturalists can sometimes provide identifications — but it's not designed for geology.

The identification algorithm (called Computer Vision) doesn't have rock identification as a core use case. You can upload a rock photo and sometimes get a useful community response, but the automated identification routinely returns plant or organism suggestions for rock photos.

If your primary interest is natural history broadly (you're as interested in the lichen on the rock as the rock itself), iNaturalist is excellent. For dedicated rock identification, it's the wrong tool.

Best for: Naturalists who occasionally find interesting rocks and want community input, not dedicated rockhounds.

4. Google Lens

Google Lens can identify common rocks and minerals as part of its general visual search capability. It pulls from Google's search index, so for well-documented specimens — common quartz varieties, granite, basalt — it works reasonably well.

The limitation is that Google Lens is a general-purpose tool. It doesn't return structured geological data. You get search results rather than identification cards. There's no Mohs hardness, no chemical formula, no crystal system — just links to web pages that may or may not contain that information.

It's a useful fallback when you're unsure what you're looking at, but it's not a substitute for a dedicated rock identifier app that returns formatted geological data.

Best for: Quick lookups when you don't have a dedicated app installed. Not a replacement for a purpose-built rock identification app.

How to Get the Best Results from Any Rock Identifier App

Regardless of which app you use, photo quality is the single biggest factor in identification accuracy. A few consistent practices improve results across all AI rock identifier apps:

  • Natural light, not flash: Flash creates specular highlights that wash out surface texture and color — exactly what the AI needs to identify the specimen. Diffuse natural light in shade works best.
  • Clean the specimen: Dirt, dust, and water change the apparent color and texture. A quick wipe improves identification accuracy significantly.
  • Show a fresh surface: Weathered surfaces often look different from the mineral's actual color and luster. Breaking the specimen to expose a fresh face, or photographing an existing break, gives the AI better data.
  • Fill the frame: Get close enough that the rock fills most of the photo. The AI needs to see surface texture and crystal structure, not a rock sitting on a background.
  • Multiple angles for crystal specimens: Crystals with distinctive habits (like pyrite cubes or quartz prisms) benefit from a second photo showing the crystal form rather than just the face.
